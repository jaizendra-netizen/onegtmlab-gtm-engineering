Three signs your CRM has stopped being the source of truth

Every sales team starts out trusting its CRM. A year or two later, reps open three other tabs before every call, the weekly pipeline review begins with an argument about whose numbers are right, and nobody is quite sure when the trust went. It rarely breaks in one dramatic moment. It erodes through small inconveniences that people route around, until the CRM is a place where records go to be forgotten.

Revenue operations is the work of reversing that erosion. Here are three signs it has already happened, and what fixing each one involves in practice.

Sign one: the same company appears three times

Open your account list and search for any large customer. If it shows up as "Acme", "Acme Inc" and "Acme Corporation", you have a duplicate problem, and it is more expensive than it looks. Activity is split across the copies, so no single record tells the real story of the relationship. Routing rules send leads from the same company to different owners. Reports undercount accounts and overcount pipeline.

The cause is usually ordinary. Imports from events, tools that create companies automatically from an email domain, and reps adding records by hand all write to the same table with no shared rule for what counts as the same company.

The fix starts with a stable key. Companies are best matched on domain and contacts on email, because names drift and domains do not. Matching on those keys lets you merge duplicates safely and, more importantly, stops new ones from appearing. Merging without fixing the intake just resets the clock.

Sign two: signals never reach the person who should act

Most B2B teams now own tools that notice useful things: a funding round, a hiring spree, a visit to the pricing page, a champion changing jobs. The failure is rarely in detecting the event. It is in what happens next. The alert lands in a shared channel, a dashboard nobody opens, or an inbox that is already full.

For a signal to be worth anything it has to become a task for a named person, with the context needed to act. That takes routing rules: score the lead or account, decide who owns it, and put it in a ranked queue so reps work the best opportunities first rather than the newest. It also takes enrichment that keeps running, because a record that was complete in January can be wrong by June.

If you recognise this sign, the question to ask is not "which tool are we missing?" It is "when a signal fires today, who sees it, and how long does it take them?" If nobody can answer, the routing layer is the gap.

Sign three: sales and marketing report different numbers

The third sign is the easiest to spot and the hardest to fix with a dashboard. Marketing says it produced a certain number of qualified leads. Sales counts fewer. Both are right, because each team is using its own definition of the stage.

This is an agreement problem before it is a data problem. Lifecycle stages from MQL through to opportunity need to be defined once, written down, and implemented the same way in the CRM and in the reports. Source fields need the same treatment, so every meeting can be traced back to the campaign or signal that started it. Only after that does a shared dashboard mean anything. A dashboard built on conflicting definitions simply automates the argument.

What a sensible RevOps engagement looks like

The pattern that holds up is the same in most CRMs, and it is worth knowing even if you never hire anyone to do it.

  1. Audit. Map the CRM, the tools around it and the data flows between them. List what is broken before touching anything.
  2. Clean. Merge duplicates, fill the gaps in the fields sales relies on, and connect enrichment and outreach tools.
  3. Automate. Turn routing, enrichment and stage updates into workflows that run without a person remembering to do them.
  4. Report and hand over. Set up the dashboards and definitions, document every workflow, and leave the team able to read and run what was built.

Two details matter more than the sequence. First, work inside the CRM you already use rather than replacing it, because a migration adds risk without fixing the habits that caused the mess. Second, check every change in the CRM after it is made. Silent failures in automated workflows are common, and the only defence is looking.

Where to go from here

If two or more of the signs above sound familiar, start with a small audit of your own. Pick fifty accounts, count the duplicates, and trace one recent signal from detection to a rep's task list. You will learn quickly where the gap is.
