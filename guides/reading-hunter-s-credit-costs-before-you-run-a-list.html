Reading Hunter's credit costs before you run a list

Before you point an email finder at a spreadsheet of domains, know what each call costs and what happens at a limit. Hunter prices in credits, and the details are spread across a pricing page, an API reference and a few footnotes. The Hunter API review with pricing, rate limits and MCP notes pulls them into one place, verified on 25 Oct 2026. Here is how I would read it.

What a credit buys

The page lists the actions you pay for, as Hunter prices them. The ones most teams touch:

  • Domain Search or Email Finder: 1 credit per email found.
  • Bulk Domain Search: 1 credit per up to 10 emails found.
  • Email Verifier: 0.5 credit per verified email, and unknown results are free.
  • Person or company enrichment: 0.2 credit per call.
  • Discover, Email Count, Lookalikes, Domain Finder and account or usage calls: free.

The page says an email not found is free, and repeated searches or verifications of the same item count once per billing period.

One wording difference is worth a quick test on your own account. The pricing FAQ says a domain search costs 1 credit per email found, while the API reference says a query is counted per call that returns at least one result. The review suggests checking before a large run.

Which plan covers your volume

The page shows both monthly and annual billing, in US dollars as Hunter's pricing page showed them on 25 Oct 2026. The Free plan gives 50 credits a month and API access. Starter is $49 a month billed monthly or $34 billed annually, with 2,000 credits a month and 3 email accounts. Growth is $149 monthly or $104 annually, with 10,000 credits a month. Scale is $299 monthly or $209 annually, with 25,000 credits a month and an account manager. Enterprise is a custom quote.

Monthly credits reset and do not roll over, while annual plans get the year's credits upfront. Older accounts may have separate searches and verifications quotas instead of one credit pool.

There is also an API-only Data Platform option: a prepaid pack, with search and verification credits priced separately on a volume slider, usable for 12 months. The page notes the calculator's default display is a static placeholder.

Rate limits and the status code that confuses people

Limits are per key. Domain Search, Email Finder and the enrichment calls allow 15 requests a second and 500 a minute. The Email Verifier allows 10 a second and 300 a minute. Discover allows 5 a second and 50 a minute.

The trap is in the error codes. Hitting the rate limit returns 403, not 429. A 429 means you have run out of credits or usage. A 451 means the person asked not to be processed, and the page says not to process that address yourself. Retry logic that treats every error alike will back off when it should stop.

If the verifier has not finished after 20 seconds it returns 202, and you poll the same call, which is counted once.

Getting a key without spending anything

The Free plan includes API access. The review notes that signup and login sit behind a Cloudflare Turnstile, and that the 50 free credits arrive only after you click the email confirmation link, which is valid for 24 hours.

The page also describes a test key, "test-api-key", that returns fixed dummy "Pied Piper" data on Domain Search, Email Finder and Email Verifier whatever the input. It is useful for wiring an integration at zero cost, but it tests plumbing only, not parsing of real data. It works on REST only; the MCP rejects it with 401.

Send the real key in the X-API-KEY header, which the docs prefer, and keep it in an environment variable.

Where it fits and where it does not

The review says Hunter is best for domain search, meaning every published email at a company with sources and confidence. It lists a cheap email finder step early in a waterfall, verification at 0.5 credit per check, free company discovery, and an official hosted MCP at mcp.hunter.io that uses OAuth or an X-API-Key header.

On the other side, it lists phone and mobile data (there is no dedicated phone finder), people search by title at scale, signed webhooks for enrichment jobs, and rolling unused monthly credits forward. Its webhooks cover only sequence message events and are unsigned, so the page suggests putting a random token in the target URL path.

A pre-flight list

  1. Decide whether your job is domain-first or person-first.
  2. Test your billing assumptions on a handful of domains.
  3. Handle 403, 429 and 451 as three different cases.
  4. Wire the integration with the test key first.
  5. Keep verification as a separate, budgeted step.

For the full endpoint list and the remaining caveats, see the review itself, and browse the wider tool and API directory if you are comparing finders.
