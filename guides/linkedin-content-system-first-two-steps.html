Start a LinkedIn content system with two decisions: audience and funnel

Most B2B teams treat LinkedIn like a lottery. They post when they remember, hope the algorithm helps, and have no mechanism for turning a like into a sales conversation. A content distribution system fixes that by putting the decisions in writing before any software is set up. The first phase has two steps, and everything else depends on them.

Step one: define the audience and the positioning

You cannot sell a solution if you do not know who has the problem. Document three things.

  1. Buyer personas. Not "marketing professionals" but exact roles, the size of company they work in and the budget they control.
  2. Pain points and objections. Use the words buyers use. A pain point might be a falling open rate on outbound email. An objection might be "we tried an agency before and they just sent spam to our buyers".
  3. Positioning. One sentence on why your product is worth buying. For example, reaching buyers who are already engaging with industry topics, so outreach never feels like cold spam.

Keep this in a permanent document that the whole team can find. To get the buyer's own language, read or listen to recordings of calls with your best customers and pull out the exact words, slang and complaints they use. If you skip this step, your copy will sound generic, and generic copy gets ignored by senior decision makers.

Step two: map content to the buying journey

A buyer rarely purchases the first time they see a post. Plan content for each stage of the journey: top of funnel, middle of funnel and bottom of funnel.

  • Top of funnel: attract. The goal is broad views and profile visits. Topics are general industry observations, common mistakes and strong opinions.
  • Middle of funnel: build authority. The goal is to show that you know how to solve the problem, step by step.
  • Bottom of funnel: convert. The goal is to give a reader who is already convinced a clear next step.

Rotate through the three on purpose. A feed of only top-of-funnel opinions builds an audience but not a pipeline, and a feed of only offers is ignored.

Why the order matters

It is tempting to start with a scheduling tool and a content calendar. But a calendar without a defined audience produces consistent posting aimed at nobody. Settling the audience and the funnel first means every later step, from research to writing to measuring engagement, has something to be checked against.

A quick check before you move on

Before you build anything else, test the two documents on someone outside the team. Ask them to read the persona and say who it describes, then give them three post ideas and ask which funnel stage each belongs to. If they hesitate, the definitions are not specific enough yet. This takes minutes and saves weeks of content aimed at the wrong reader. Revisit both documents whenever a new customer call changes how you understand the problem.

What comes after

Once these two steps are written down, the later phases of a content system can be built on them: turning market research into topics, systematising how posts are created, and noticing who engages so that the right people get a follow-up. Each phase has its own steps and tools, and each should be tied back to the personas and funnel stages you chose here.
